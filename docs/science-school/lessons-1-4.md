# Science School — the first four lessons (draft card text)

Sophie asked for a **literal science school**, built to look exactly like Witch
School (same tap-through cards, same progress dashes, same quiz + After card)
with its own colour palette. This is the draft text for the first four lessons
of the **Biology** path, written before any art so the words can be judged on
their own.

**Nothing here is wired in yet.** No `LESSONS` entry, no tile, no art. Sophie
reads this first, then the approved cards get illustrated and wired.

## What changes from Witch School (and what doesn't)

Same: the card shape (`{ img, bg, kicker, h, body }`), 10-is-a-floor card
counts, one idea per card, a `know` quiz placed after the facts it tests, an
**After** card last.

Different, and deliberately:

- **The After card is an OBSERVATION, not a feature.** Witch School's last card
  points at something the app can do (draw a card, open the Book of Shadows). A
  science lesson has no equivalent, so its "Tonight" is something real the
  reader can go and look at, count, or notice that week. None of these four
  carry a CTA.
- **Every lesson names the experiment.** In science the evidence *is* the
  content, so each lesson has at least one card that answers "how do we know
  that?" with a person, a date, and what they actually did. The honesty card is
  the spine here, not the exception it is in Witch School.
- **The voice rules carry over unchanged** — no AI-tells, no mic-drop closers,
  no profound-simplicity pronouncements, aspirational not consoling. See
  `docs/witch-school-lessons.md`. The "never adjudicate the craft against
  science" rule is Witch-School-only and does not apply here; this school IS the
  science. It still must not sneer at anything.

## Lesson 1 — The Cell

*Course opener. Everything after this stands on it.*
Suggested prefix `ce-` is taken by Crystal Energy — use **`cl-`**.

**1. kicker `The Cell` · h `Everything is made of these`**
Every living thing you have ever seen — the oak, the mould on the bread, the
person next to you — is either one cell or a great many stuck together. There
is no exception anyone has found. This is the first lesson because the next
three are all about what happens inside one. We'll go one idea at a time.

**2. kicker `London, 1665` · h `A slice of cork`**
**Robert Hooke** put a shaving of cork under his own microscope and saw it was
not solid at all: it was a honeycomb of tiny empty boxes. He needed a word, and
the boxes reminded him of the small bare rooms monks slept in — *cells*. He
published the drawing in <i>Micrographia</i> the same year. He was looking at
the dead walls of plant cells, which is why they looked empty.

**3. kicker `Delft, 1670s` · h `The first living ones`**
**Antonie van Leeuwenhoek** ground his own lenses, better than anyone else's,
and turned them on pond water, scrapings from his teeth, rainwater from a gutter.
He found things swimming. He called them <b>animalcules</b> and wrote to the
Royal Society about them for fifty years. Nobody had known that a drop of water
was full of life.

**4. kicker `The scale` · h `How small, exactly`**
A typical cell in your body is about a hundredth of a millimetre across — you
could line up roughly a hundred of them along the edge of a grain of sand. That
is why nobody knew about them until lenses got good. Not every cell is small: a
single nerve cell can run from the base of your spine to your toe, and an
ostrich egg is one cell.

**5. kicker `The count` · h `Thirty-seven trillion`**
The most careful estimate of how many cells a human body contains — done by
adding up organ by organ rather than guessing — is about <b>37 trillion</b>
(Bianconi and colleagues, 2013). Written out, that is 37 followed by twelve
zeroes. They are being replaced constantly; the estimate is a snapshot of a
population, not a fixed number.

**6. kicker `The correction` · h `You are not outnumbered ten to one`**
You will still read that bacterial cells outnumber your own by ten to one. That
figure came from a back-of-envelope calculation in 1972 and was repeated for
forty years. When it was finally checked properly (**Sender, Fuchs and Milo,
2016**) the real ratio came out at roughly <b>one to one</b> — about 38 trillion
bacteria to 30 trillion human cells. A number can survive a long time on being
repeated.

**7. kicker `The rule` · h `Cells come from cells`**
Schleiden and Schwann proposed in 1838–39 that all plants and all animals are
made of cells. **Rudolf Virchow** added the part that mattered most in 1855:
<i>omnis cellula e cellula</i> — every cell comes from a cell that was already
alive. No cell has ever been seen to assemble itself from scratch. Your cells
are an unbroken line of division going back billions of years.

**8. QUIZ — kicker `Try it` · h `Check your knowing`**
- *Hooke named the cell after…* → **the small rooms monks slept in** ·
  *He was looking at dead cork, a honeycomb of empty boxes, and reached for a
  word that fit the shape.*
- *The bacteria-to-human-cell ratio is closest to…* → **one to one** ·
  *The famous ten-to-one came from a rough 1972 estimate and stood unchecked for
  forty years. Measured properly in 2016: about 38 trillion to 30 trillion.*
- *Virchow's addition to cell theory was that…* → **every cell comes from
  another cell** · *Not that cells exist — that none has ever been seen to
  start from nothing.*

**9. kicker `The exception` · h `The things that aren't cells`**
A virus is not a cell. It has genetic material and a coat and nothing else — no
machinery of its own, no way to make a copy of itself without borrowing a cell's
equipment. This is why "is a virus alive?" has no agreed answer: it fails the
definition biology built around the cell.

**10. kicker `After` · h `Tonight` ✦**
Look at something living with your eyes as close as they will go — the edge of a
leaf, the skin on your forearm, a slice of onion held to a window. You will not
see cells. What you *can* see is the point: this whole layer of the world was
invisible until 1665, and everything in the next three lessons happens down
there.<br><br>**Tonight:** find the smallest living thing you can actually see.
✦

## Lesson 2 — What's Inside One

Prefix **`in-`**.

**1. kicker `Inside a cell` · h `Not a bag of soup`**
A cell is not one substance. Inside its outer membrane there are separate pieces
of machinery, each walled off, each doing one job — the way a kitchen has an
oven and a sink and a bin rather than one all-purpose surface. They are called
<b>organelles</b>, little organs.

**2. kicker `The office` · h `The nucleus`**
The largest thing in most of your cells is the <b>nucleus</b>, a sphere with its
own double membrane holding the DNA. Nothing comes out of it but copies —
instructions are transcribed inside and the copy is sent out to be used. The
original never leaves the room. Lesson 4 is about what happens to those copies.

**3. kicker `The floor` · h `Ribosomes`**
<b>Ribosomes</b> are the machines that build proteins, and there are millions of
them in a single cell — some drifting loose, some studding the folded membranes
around the nucleus. They are the smallest thing in this lesson and the busiest.

**4. kicker `The power` · h `Mitochondria`**
<b>Mitochondria</b> are bean-shaped and there may be a handful in one cell or
thousands, depending on how hard it works — heart muscle is packed with them.
They take sugar and oxygen and produce the chemical the rest of the cell spends,
ATP. The next card is why they are strange.

**5. kicker `The strange part` · h `They have their own DNA`**
Mitochondria carry their <b>own DNA</b>, separate from the DNA in the nucleus —
in humans a small circular loop of 16,569 letters. They have their own
ribosomes. They divide on their own schedule rather than when the cell does. And
you inherited yours from your mother only; your father's contributed nothing.
None of that is how an organelle should behave.

**6. kicker `Boston, 1967` · h `The paper nobody would take`**
**Lynn Margulis** had an explanation: mitochondria were once free-living
bacteria that got swallowed by a larger cell and were never digested — both
sides did better, and they stayed. She wrote it up as *On the Origin of Mitosing
Cells*. It was rejected by <b>fifteen journals</b>, some without being read,
before the *Journal of Theoretical Biology* published it. She was thirty and
publishing under a married name, Lynn Sagan.

**7. kicker `The verdict` · h `She was right`**
When DNA sequencing arrived it settled the argument. Read a mitochondrion's
genes and its closest living relatives are not any part of you — they are a
group of bacteria. The same is true of chloroplasts, the green parts of plants,
which were once free-living photosynthetic bacteria. <b>Endosymbiosis</b> is now
in every textbook.

**8. QUIZ — kicker `Try it` · h `Check your knowing`**
- *Which of these is the giveaway that mitochondria were once separate
  organisms?* → **they have their own DNA and divide on their own schedule** ·
  *A normal organelle is built by the cell to order. These carry their own
  genes, their own ribosomes, and their own timing.*
- *Margulis's paper was rejected by…* → **fifteen journals** · *Some without
  reading it. The Journal of Theoretical Biology published it in 1967.*
- *You inherit your mitochondria from…* → **your mother only** · *Which is why
  mitochondrial DNA is used to trace maternal lines back through thousands of
  years.*

**9. kicker `The consequence` · h `Two kinds of cell`**
Cells that keep their DNA in a nucleus are called <b>eukaryotes</b> — you,
oak trees, yeast, amoebae. Cells that don't are <b>prokaryotes</b> — bacteria
and archaea, which are older and enormously more numerous. The swallowing
Margulis described is roughly where the first group came from.

**10. kicker `After` · h `Tonight` ✦**
Mitochondria are the reason you breathe. Every breath you take is oxygen going
to trillions of former bacteria so they can finish burning your last meal, and
the carbon dioxide coming back out is their exhaust. Notice one breath with that
in mind.<br><br>**Tonight:** one breath, followed all the way down. ✦

## Lesson 3 — DNA

Prefix **`dn-`**.

**1. kicker `DNA` · h `The instructions`**
Every cell in your body carries the same set of instructions, written in a
molecule called <b>DNA</b>, and the copy in a cell in your foot is identical to
the copy in a cell behind your eye. What differs is which parts each cell is
reading. This lesson is what the molecule is; the next is how a cell reads it.

**2. kicker `Tübingen, 1869` · h `Found in bandages`**
**Friedrich Miescher** was studying white blood cells and getting them from the
pus-soaked bandages of a nearby hospital. He isolated something from the nuclei
that was not protein and not fat, and called it <b>nuclein</b>. That was DNA,
found eighty-four years before anyone worked out what it looked like or what it
did.

**3. kicker `The doubt` · h `Nobody thought it was the answer`**
For decades DNA was assumed to be too boring to carry heredity — only four
different units, where proteins had twenty. Most biologists expected genes to
turn out to be proteins. **Avery, MacLeod and McCarty** showed in 1944 that it
was DNA after all, and the field still took years to be convinced.

**4. kicker `London, 1952` · h `Photograph 51`**
**Rosalind Franklin**'s laboratory produced an X-ray image of DNA fibres so
clean that its cross-shaped pattern could only mean a helix — and the spacing in
it gave the dimensions. The exposure took around a hundred hours. It is the
single most important piece of evidence for the structure, and it is a
photograph of something far too small to see.

**5. kicker `Cambridge, 1953` · h `The double helix`**
Watson and Crick published the structure in *Nature* in April 1953: two strands
winding round each other, joined down the middle by pairs of chemical letters.
Franklin's photograph had been shown to them without her knowledge. She died of
ovarian cancer in 1958 at 37; the Nobel was awarded in 1962, and is not given
posthumously.

**6. kicker `The letters` · h `A pairs with T`**
There are four letters: <b>A, T, C, G</b>. A only ever pairs with T, and C only
ever pairs with G. That is the whole reason the molecule can copy itself —
unzip the two strands and each one carries enough information to rebuild its
missing partner exactly. Watson and Crick's paper noted this in one famously
understated sentence.

**7. kicker `The length` · h `Two metres, coiled`**
The DNA in a single one of your cells is about <b>two metres</b> long if it were
pulled straight, wound down into a nucleus a few thousandths of a millimetre
across. It manages that by wrapping around protein spools, which is also how the
cell controls what can be read: tightly wound is unreadable, loosened is open
for business.

**8. QUIZ — kicker `Try it` · h `Check your knowing`**
- *DNA was first isolated in…* → **1869** · *By Friedrich Miescher, out of the
  pus on hospital bandages — eighty-four years before its structure was known.*
- *The evidence that settled the helix was…* → **an X-ray photograph** ·
  *Photograph 51, from Rosalind Franklin's lab, took around a hundred hours of
  exposure.*
- *A pairs only with T, and C only with G. The consequence is…* → **each strand
  contains enough to rebuild the other** · *Which is how a cell copies its
  instructions before it divides.*

**9. kicker `The size of it` · h `Three billion letters`**
Your genome is about <b>three billion</b> letter-pairs long. Printed at ordinary
book size it would run to hundreds of thousands of pages. The Human Genome
Project took thirteen years and finished a draft in 2003; the same job now takes
about a day.

**10. kicker `The surprise` · h `Most of it is not genes`**
Only about <b>1 to 2 per cent</b> of your genome is instructions for building
proteins — roughly 20,000 genes, which is around the same number a nematode worm
has. What the rest does is still being worked out: some of it controls when
genes switch on, some is the wreckage of ancient viruses, and some may do
nothing at all. Expecting "more complex animal, more genes" turned out to be
wrong.

**11. kicker `After` · h `This week` ✦**
Two metres of it in every cell, three billion letters long, and the copy in your
thumb is the same as the copy in your heart. Find one trait you share with a
parent — an eye colour, a way your hands look, the shape of an ear — and know
that you are looking at a passage of text that was copied, letter for letter,
into you.<br><br>**This week:** find the one you inherited. ✦

## Lesson 4 — Reading the Code

Prefix **`cd-`**.

**1. kicker `The code` · h `From letters to a body`**
DNA is a list of instructions, but an instruction does nothing until something
reads it. This lesson is the reading: how a sequence of four repeating letters
becomes a physical object — an enzyme, a hair, the pigment in an iris.

**2. kicker `Two steps` · h `Copy, then build`**
It happens in two moves. <b>Transcription</b>: inside the nucleus a stretch of
DNA is copied into a portable single-stranded version called mRNA, and the copy
leaves. <b>Translation</b>: out in the cell a ribosome runs along that copy and
builds a protein to match. Crick named the overall flow the <i>central dogma</i>
in 1957.

**3. kicker `The unit` · h `Three letters at a time`**
The ribosome reads in <b>threes</b>. Each group of three letters — a
<b>codon</b> — specifies one amino acid, and amino acids strung in order make a
protein. Three letters from an alphabet of four gives 64 possible codons for
20 amino acids, so most amino acids have more than one codon that calls them.

**4. kicker `Bethesda, 1961` · h `Three in the morning`**
Nobody knew which codon meant what until **Heinrich Matthaei**, working with
**Marshall Nirenberg**, made an artificial RNA of nothing but U, added it to a
cell extract, and at three in the morning on 27 May found one of his twenty
tubes had built a protein of nothing but phenylalanine. <b>UUU means
phenylalanine.</b> It was the first word anyone translated, and the rest of the
code followed within five years.

**5. kicker `The punctuation` · h `Start and stop`**
The code has punctuation. <b>AUG</b> means start — and also means the amino acid
methionine, so every protein begins with one. Three codons mean stop and specify
no amino acid at all; the ribosome reaches one and releases what it has built.

**6. kicker `The universality` · h `The same code in everything`**
Here is the part that carries the weight: the codon table is very nearly
<b>identical in every living thing</b> — bacteria, yeast, oak, whale, you. UUU
means phenylalanine in all of them. The simplest explanation is that the code
was fixed once, in a shared ancestor, and has barely moved since.

**7. kicker `The proof of it` · h `Bacteria that make insulin`**
That shared code is not a theory you have to take on trust — it is
manufacturing. In 1978 the human gene for insulin was put into <i>E. coli</i>,
and the bacteria read it and built human insulin, because to them it was just an
instruction in a language they already spoke. It reached patients in 1982. Most
insulin in the world is made this way now.

**8. QUIZ — kicker `Try it` · h `Check your knowing`**
- *A codon is…* → **three letters of DNA or RNA** · *Read in threes, each group
  naming one amino acid. Four letters in groups of three gives 64 combinations.*
- *The first codon to be cracked was…* → **UUU, in 1961** · *Matthaei and
  Nirenberg's artificial poly-U RNA built a protein of pure phenylalanine — at
  three in the morning.*
- *Bacteria can be made to produce human insulin because…* → **the genetic code
  is the same in both** · *The gene is an instruction in a language E. coli
  already reads. Human insulin has been made this way since 1982.*

**9. kicker `When it goes wrong` · h `One letter`**
Change a single letter and the effect ranges from nothing at all to everything.
Many changes land on a codon that still calls the same amino acid, and nothing
happens. Sickle cell disease is one letter — a single A becoming a T, one amino
acid swapped, and the shape of the haemoglobin molecule changes enough to
deform the whole red blood cell.

**10. kicker `After` · h `Tonight` ✦**
Your body is running this right now, in every cell, millions of times a second:
a copy leaves a nucleus, a ribosome reads it in threes, a protein comes out
folded and working. Eat something with protein in it and you are taking apart
someone else's version of the same process to build your own.<br><br>**Tonight:**
one meal, read as instructions being recycled. ✦
