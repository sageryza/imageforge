## The proposed topic menu

### Cognitive science (a three-lesson opening arc)

1. **Theory of Mind** — drafted in full below.
2. **False Memories** — Elizabeth Loftus's crash films (1974): ask "how fast
   were the cars going when they *smashed*" instead of "*hit*" and people
   remember higher speeds — and a week later remember broken glass that was
   never shown. Then "Lost in the Mall" (1995): a planted paragraph gives about
   a quarter of adults a full, detailed childhood memory of an event that never
   happened. Memory as retelling rather than playback, and why courtrooms had
   to change. (Sits nicely next to the whole memory-library world.)
3. **The Split Brain** — the epilepsy patients whose brain halves were
   surgically separated (Sperry & Gazzaniga, 1960s; Nobel 1981). Each half can
   answer questions separately — and the talking left half confidently invents
   reasons for what the silent right half just did (the chicken-claw /
   snow-shovel trial). What that says about the feeling of being one person.

### Sophie's two

4. **The Habsburgs** — two centuries of uncle-niece and first-cousin marriages
   to keep the crown in the family. The jaw that got its own name (a 2019
   study had surgeons rate the portraits — jaw severity tracks the inbreeding
   coefficient). Charles II of Spain, whose inbreeding coefficient (~0.254)
   matches what brother-sister parents would produce; half the dynasty's
   children dead before ten while ordinary Spanish villages lost about one in
   five; Joanna the Mad; the Spanish line extinct with Charles in 1700.
   Sneaky-teaches recessive genes and pedigree collapse.
5. **One Big Bed** — how humans slept before bedrooms. The Viking longhouse:
   the whole household sleeping together in the one hall around the hearth.
   Communal sleep as the human norm for most of history — strangers sharing
   inn beds, the Great Bed of Ware (built to sleep a dozen, famous enough that
   Shakespeare name-checks it in *Twelfth Night*). Then Roger Ekirch's
   discovery in old court records: "first sleep" and "second sleep," with a
   waking hour around midnight, as the ordinary pattern before artificial
   light. How recent eight-hours-alone-in-the-dark really is.

### The anything pile

6. **The Dancing Plague of 1518** — Strasbourg. Frau Troffea starts dancing in
   the street and cannot stop; within a month it is hundreds; the city's cure
   (hired musicians and a stage) makes it worse; people die of it. Mass
   psychogenic illness — half history, half cognitive science.
7. **John Snow and the Pump Handle** — the 1854 Soho cholera outbreak, the map
   of deaths, the brewery workers who stayed healthy because they drank beer
   instead of water, and the pump handle removed while official medicine still
   blamed bad air. The birth of epidemiology as a detective story.
8. **The Radium Girls** — watch-dial painters taught to point the brush with
   their lips, in an era when radium was sold as a health tonic. Grace Fryer's
   lawsuit, the jaws that broke and the graves that still read on a Geiger
   counter — and the case that invented occupational-disease law.
9. **Universe 25** — John Calhoun's mouse paradise: unlimited food, no
   predators. Population booms, then the "beautiful ones" who only groom, then
   extinction with resources still full. Plus the honest card: how far the
   internet has oversold it as human prophecy, and what it actually showed.
10. **The Longitude Prize** — a 1707 fleet wrecks on the Scilly Isles because
    nobody at sea can know their longitude; Parliament posts a £20,000 prize;
    a self-taught carpenter named John Harrison spends forty years building
    sea-clocks the astronomers keep refusing to accept, until the King
    intervenes.
11. **The Antikythera Mechanism** — sponge divers, 1901, pull a corroded lump
    off a Roman shipwreck. Decades later it turns out to be a geared bronze
    computer from ~100 BC that predicted eclipses and tracked calendars —
    nothing of its complexity appears again for 1,400 years. CT scanners are
    still reading text off its gears.
12. **Ergot in Salem?** — 1976: a graduate student proposes that moldy rye
    (whose fungus makes LSD-like alkaloids) explains the Salem girls'
    convulsions. Why the theory caught fire in the press, why historians
    knocked it down, and what the accusations actually looked like up close.
    A witch-history bridge, honesty-lesson shaped.

## Lesson — Theory of Mind

*Cognitive science, lesson 1.* Prefix **`tm-`** (free; `cl- in- dn- cd-` are
the science prefixes taken so far, plus the Witch School list).

**1. kicker `Theory of Mind` · h `Two pictures at once`**
Think of the last surprise party you kept quiet about, or a spoiler you
swallowed. To pull that off you held two pictures of the world at the same
time — the true one, and the wrong one in someone else's head — and steered by
both. Psychologists call that ability <b>theory of mind</b>. This lesson is
where the idea came from, the strange cliff in childhood where it switches on,
and the forty-year argument about who else has it. One idea at a time.

**2. kicker `Pennsylvania, 1978` · h `Sarah watches a man struggle`**
**David Premack and Guy Woodruff** showed videotapes to <b>Sarah</b>, a
chimpanzee, of a man struggling: locked in a cage, shivering beside an unlit
heater, stuck with an unplugged record player. Offered photographs, Sarah kept
choosing the solution — the key, the lit wick, the plugged-in cord — as if she
understood what the man <i>wanted</i>. Their paper asked "Does the chimpanzee
have a theory of mind?", and the word <i>theory</i> was chosen with care: a
want or a belief cannot be seen, only inferred from behaviour, the way a
scientist works with invisible things.
- faq: *Who was Sarah?* → An adult chimp raised around people, already famous
  from Premack\'s language experiments — she "wrote" with plastic tokens on a
  board.
- faq: *Did she pass everything?* → Nearly every trial. But sceptics pointed
  out she might just know that keys go with locks from experience, no thought
  about the man\'s mind required — and that objection is the next card.

**3. kicker `The objection` · h `Only a wrong belief can prove it`**
The journal printed replies alongside the paper, and the philosophers —
**Daniel Dennett** among them — converged on one point. As long as what the
other fellow believes is <i>true</i>, you can predict him without touching his
mind at all: just predict the world. The clean test is a <b>false belief</b> —
to guess where a man will look for a thing that was moved behind his back, you
must carry his out-of-date picture of the world separately from your own. That
one paragraph of criticism designed the next forty years of experiments.

**4. kicker `Salzburg, 1983` · h `Maxi's chocolate`**
**Heinz Wimmer and Josef Perner** staged a puppet show for children. Maxi puts
his chocolate in the <b>blue</b> cupboard and goes out to play; while he is
gone, his mother moves it to the <b>green</b> one for her baking. Where will
Maxi look when he comes back? An adult can barely feel it as a question.
Three-year-olds answered <i>green</i> — where the chocolate really is — and
answered with total confidence. The flip to blue arrives around the fourth
birthday, in country after country where the test has been run.

**5. kicker `London, 1985` · h `Sally and Anne`**
**Simon Baron-Cohen, Alan Leslie and Uta Frith** shrank the test to two dolls.
Sally hides a marble in her basket and leaves; Anne moves it to her own box;
where will Sally look? <b>85%</b> of typically developing children passed, and
so did 86% of children with Down syndrome — but only <b>20%</b> of the
autistic children, most of them older, with higher measured IQs than the other
groups. The result made Sally-Anne one of the most famous tests in psychology,
and started a debate it took decades to straighten out — two cards from now.

**6. kicker `The pencil trick` · h `Your own mind, thirty seconds ago`**
Show a three-year-old a Smarties tube and ask what\'s inside: "Smarties." Open
it — it holds pencils. Now ask what they thought was inside <i>before</i> it
was opened: "<b>Pencils.</b> I always knew." Ask what the next child will
guess: "pencils" (Perner, Leekam and Wimmer, 1987; Gopnik and Astington,
1988). At three, a false belief cannot be reconstructed even when it was your
own, moments earlier. Whatever arrives at four covers your own past mind too.

**7. QUIZ — kicker `Try it` · h `Check your knowing`**
- *It is called a "theory" of mind because…* → **beliefs and wants can\'t be
  seen, only inferred** · *You never observe a belief. You build a theory of
  invisible things from what people do, and predict with it.*
- *Asked where Maxi will look for his moved chocolate, three-year-olds point
  to…* → **the cupboard where it really is** · *What they know themselves,
  they hand to everyone else. The flip comes around the fourth birthday.*
- *Shown that the Smarties tube holds pencils, a three-year-old says they
  originally thought it held…* → **pencils — and that they always knew** ·
  *A false belief can\'t be reconstructed at three, even about your own mind.*

**8. kicker `The babies` · h `Fifteen months old, still arguing`**
In 2005 **Kristine Onishi and Renée Baillargeon** ran a wordless version on
15-month-olds. Babies watched an actor reach for a toy she held a false belief
about — and stared reliably longer when her reach didn\'t match her belief.
Staring longer is the infant lab\'s standard signal for "that broke my
expectation." So the four-year cliff may be about handling the <i>question</i>
— language, pointing, juggling two answers — while some tracking of other
minds runs far earlier. Fair warning: replications of the baby work have gone
both ways, and the field is still openly fighting about it.
- faq: *How is staring data?* → Infants look longer at events that violate
  what they expected — a measure calibrated over decades of vision and physics
  studies before it was pointed at beliefs.
- faq: *What does "gone both ways" mean?* → Some labs re-running the infant
  studies get the effect and others don\'t, including a large multi-lab
  attempt in 2018. Nobody disputes the four-year-old results; the fight is
  over how much the babies are really doing.

**9. kicker `The correction` · h `It fails in both directions`**
The 1985 autism result hardened into a strong claim — "mindblindness," a
missing mind-reading module. **Damian Milton**, an autistic sociologist,
published the correction in 2012: run the test the other way round and
non-autistic people are just as poor at reading autistic minds — they misjudge
autistic expressions and miss autistic meaning at the same rates. He called it
the <b>double empathy problem</b>: two different kinds of mind, each opaque to
the other, with the breakdown sitting between them rather than inside one of
them. A decade of studies since has largely backed him.

**10. kicker `Vienna, 2016` · h `The raven and the peephole`**
Ravens hide food for later and rob each other\'s caches, so a raven that
believes it is watched hides differently — quicker, farther off, ready to
re-hide. **Thomas Bugnyar** gave ravens a room with a peephole into the next
chamber and let each bird use the hole itself. Later, alone — no raven in
sight — he played raven sounds from next door. With the hole open, the birds
guarded their caches as if watched; with it covered, they relaxed. From their
own remembered seeing, they had imagined an unseen watcher doing the same.

**11. kicker `Leipzig & Kyoto, 2016` · h `The man in the ape suit`**
**Christopher Krupenye and Fumihiro Kano** sat chimpanzees, bonobos and
orangutans in front of short films: a man competes with a costumed "King Kong"
who moves a hidden object while the man is away. An infrared eye-tracker
watched the apes watch. When the man returned holding a false belief, their
gaze ran <i>ahead</i> of him — to the empty place where he wrongly believed
the thing was. It is the same anticipatory-looking test used on human infants,
passed the same way. Thirty-eight years on, the best answer to Premack\'s
title is: probably — at least in the way a baby has one.

**12. kicker `After` · h `Tonight` ✦**
Every thriller runs on you doing, effortlessly, what this lesson is about.
Alfred Hitchcock explained it to François Truffaut with a bomb under a café
table: keep it secret and the blast buys fifteen seconds of surprise; show the
audience first and the same scene becomes fifteen minutes of suspense, powered
entirely by a false belief you are holding on the characters\' behalf.
<br><br><b>Tonight:</b> in whatever you watch, catch one scene that only works
because you know something a character doesn\'t — then count how many minds
the story is asking you to run at once. ✦
