## Chapter Two

The woman took the phone out of my hand.

Actually that's not right either. What happened was, the dispatcher asked for the cross street and I didn't know it — four months I'd been coming to this café and I had never once looked up at a sign — and the woman said it. Quietly, from behind me. "Alta and Fourth," like she was telling me the time. So I repeated it into the phone as if it were mine, and then the dispatcher wanted to know if he was breathing, and I said "Are you breathing?" to the man on the ground, and he said "Robert Pattinson" again, which I decided to count as a yes.

Twenty minutes, the dispatcher said. There had been a pileup on the 10.

Twenty minutes. In twenty minutes there would be a crowd. In twenty minutes someone would have a phone out — not this man's phone, which was in pieces, but a working one — and by tonight there would be a picture of me crouched in the road in these sweatpants, under a headline with a pun in it. (I had a vision of the pun. It involved the word "hero" and my surname. It was not a good pun.)

"I can take him," the woman said again.

Her car was right there. That was the thing. A little grey hatchback with a dent in the door {a bumper sticker about a dog}, parked with two wheels up on the curb like she'd abandoned it mid-thought, and it was right there, and the man was asking if he was in heaven for the third time, and I have never been any good at waiting.

"Fine," I said. "Fine. Get his legs."

She did not get his legs. She got his one shoe, which had come off, and held it. I got his legs and his arms and most of his middle, and the woman opened the back door and stood there holding the shoe while I folded a grown man into a hatchback. He wasn't heavy. He was making a small noise, like a kettle a long way off.

"Watch his head," she said.

"I am watching his head."

"You're not, you're — there. There." She reached in past me and put her cardigan under it, and for a second we were both inside the back of her car with our faces about four inches apart, and she went a colour I would not have thought a person could go. Then she straightened up and hit her head on the door frame and did not say one word about it.

I got in the back with him. I don't know why. I think I thought someone should hold his head, and she was driving. The waistband man's eyes were open and he was looking up at me with an expression of enormous peace.

"You're taller in person," he said.

"Thank you."

"No, I mean — I always thought you'd be shorter."

"Okay."

She drove very carefully. That was the first thing I noticed about her driving: she used her signal to change lanes on an empty road. Both hands on the wheel, ten and two, and she looked in the rearview mirror a great deal — not at the road behind us, I realised after the second light. At me.

"Eyes front," I said.

"Sorry."

"It's fine. Just — the road."

"Sorry." And then, about a block later, in a completely different voice, small and thrilled: "You said thank you to him."

"What?"

"When he said you were tall. You said thank you. That's so —" and she didn't finish, just shook her head at the windshield as though the windshield had said something she agreed with.

The man in my lap said, "Is she your girlfriend?"

"No."

"She looks like she could be your girlfriend."

"She's a — she's a stranger who has a car."

"Oh," he said, and then, thoughtfully, "you should get her number," and closed his eyes, and I thought for one very long second that he had died and I had killed him with my sweatpants. But he was only asleep.

## Chapter Three

The hospital took him. That was the whole of my involvement, in the end. Two people in blue came out with a wheeled bed and took him off my lap and rolled him through a pair of doors that hissed shut behind him, and a woman behind glass asked me his name and I said I didn't know it, and she asked if I was family and I said no, and she asked for my name and I said —

"Rob," I said. "Robert."

"Last name?"

"Patterson." It was the barista's. It was the first one I thought of. She wrote it down without looking up, which is the great blessing of an emergency room: nobody in one has the slightest interest in you unless you're bleeding.

Then I was outside again, under the awning, and it was a completely ordinary afternoon. Somewhere behind me a man with his waistband inside out was being x-rayed. My latte was on a table two miles away, going cold, next to my glasses and my cap, and Robert Patterson was probably wondering whether I'd walked out on the bill. I patted my pockets. Phone. Card. Nothing to put on my head.

The grey car was still there.

She hadn't gone. She was sitting in it with the engine off and both hands on the wheel, ten and two, looking straight ahead at nothing, like she was waiting on a light that was never going to change. When I looked at her she looked away. Then she looked back. Then she leaned across and pushed the passenger door open from the inside, and it swung out and bumped, gently, against my knee.

I want to be clear that I got in the car on purpose. Nobody made me. I got in because my glasses were at the café and my face was on the outside of my head and this woman had already seen it, so there was nothing left to lose with her — and because I have never learned to say no to a door that's already open. Also, and I would only admit this to a stranger: I wanted to know what she was going to say. The whole drive, she'd said maybe eleven words. There was something in her she was sitting on.

"The café," I said. "Please. Where you found me."

She nodded. She started the car. She used her signal.

We went past the café.

I saw it go by — the awning, my table, and yes, there was Robert Patterson in the window with a rag, looking out at the street like he'd lost something — and I said "That was it," and she said "I know," and kept driving.

"You can — you can stop here, this is —"

"I know."

"Then —"

The locks went down. All four of them, with that little chunk they make. She'd done it with her elbow, I think, not even looking, and then she reached over and turned the radio on, and up, and up, and it was a song I hated, and we were on the freeway.

## Chapter Four

*(Her pages go here first — "Are you…kidnapping me?" through the waves. Then:)*

She got off the freeway at the coast. The waves were the first thing I understood about where we were going, and the second was that we weren't going far — she drove like someone who'd driven this road a hundred times and had only ever been to one place on it. She slowed at every turnout as if considering, and didn't take any of them. She was working up to something. You could see it in her hands.

"You're going to kill me," I said, conversationally, to the window.

"No!" It came out of her so fast and so loud that she swerved. "No. God. No, I'm not going to — no."

"Then what."

"I'm going to —" She swallowed. "Lunch," she said. "I'm going to buy you lunch."

It was a fish place. A shack, really, on the land side of the highway, hand-painted sign, picnic tables, a line — there is always a line — and she parked in the dirt and turned off the engine and did not unlock the doors.

"I have to tell you something," she said, "and I need you to not leave until I've told you."

"That's — that's what kidnapping is."

"I know what kidnapping is."

"Okay."

"I have a whole speech."

"Okay."

"And I have to do it in order, or it won't make sense."

Here is what I have never told anyone about that afternoon: I could have got out. The lock on my side was a little post, and I had my hand on it, and I could have pulled it up and walked across the highway and stuck my thumb out and been home in an hour, and she would not have followed me — I could see that. She was more frightened of me than I was of her. She was holding the wheel like it was the only thing keeping her in the car.

Instead I said, "Do they have tea?"

She unlocked the doors.

They did have tea, as it turned out, in a metal pot, at a fish place, and I ordered it because I didn't want to be seen eating in the sweatpants, and she ordered fish and chips and then didn't eat any of it. We sat at the far end of one of the tables, as far from the line as we could get. A gull stood on the next table and watched me with one eye. Three people in the line looked at me twice. None of them looked a third time. The sweatpants were doing their job.

She had her speech in her head; I could see her reading it off the inside of her forehead. She started it four times. Once she got as far as "When I was" and stopped. Once she got as far as "You know how" and stopped. Once she just opened her mouth and a small dry sound came out and she picked up her tea and put it down again.

"Take your time," I said, which was not what I meant. What I meant was hurry up.

The fourth time, she looked at her hands and said, "I didn't want to tell you. I wanted you to fall in love with me. For me."
