# Robert Pattinson — the ending (chapters 8–9), draft 2

Redone after her notes on draft 1: the wish is an experiment that runs on both of them and lands crooked, the way her wishes always do; the rain is hers; he keeps finding her disgusting (that's the joke); the supermarket is the whole world for now; and underneath it, the girl who decided at nine that she could never want anything. Picks up after "Thank you," he said, a smile playing at his lips.

## Chapter Eight

Nothing happened, and then something did, and I didn't like it.

The candle went out — she'd blown it — and the smoke went up and joined the general Ralphs air, and I held my hands up in the light from the freezer cases to see if they'd changed. Same hands. Same long, famous, ridiculous fingers. I felt exactly as likeable as I had a minute ago, which is to say I had no idea.

"Well," I said.

"It's not always right away," she said. "Sometimes it's a day —"

"A week, yes."

"Or it's already happened and you just don't know yet."

I ate the cupcake. It tasted like the cardboard it came in and I ate it in three bites and licked the paper, and she ate one too, with the paper still on, and got a bit of it in her teeth, and looked at me the whole time the way a dog looks at a door.

A supermarket at night is a strange place to be locked in with a person you'd rather not be. It hums. Every so often the tinny voice came on to tell us about the flood, and then it went off, and the hum came back. We walked the aisles because there was nothing else to do — me in front, her two steps behind like a cart I was pulling — past twelve kinds of mustard and a whole wall of paper towels and a bin of DVDs where I found, third from the top, my own face, looking wet and concerned, for four dollars. I put it back face down. She saw me do it and said nothing, and picked at her thumbnail, and I heard it.

The guard came round at midnight. He told us. "Midnight," he said, standing over us where we'd ended up, on the floor between the juice and the graham crackers, with twenty-two cupcakes between us like a campfire. He looked at me a long time. I waited for the Cullen thing. The daughter thing.

"My daughter's got your face on her wall," he said, slow, working it out as he went. "Big poster. Over the bed." He shook his head. "I never thought about it before. A grown man. Over a kid's bed. That's a bit much, isn't it." He wasn't asking. "I'm going to take that down when I get home."

And off he went, and I sat there with icing on my chin and felt — it was what I'd asked for. Heads turning. I'd been wrong about what it would feel like, that's all. I'd wanted to be hated for nothing, the way you'd hate a smell. This was being looked at properly and hated for what was there. This was accurate. I hadn't ordered accurate.

She'd fallen asleep against the apple juice with her mouth open and one hand palm-up on the floor, like she was waiting for someone to put something in it. There were two of her fingernails on the linoleum by my knee. I moved my knee.

I should say something about the rain, because I worked it out about three in the morning and I'd like the credit. It hadn't been raining when we went into the fish place. It started around the time she said she'd wanted to see me, and it was buckets by the time she said, out loud, on this floor, "I wish this moment would never end," and then there was a flood warning and a palm frond in the door. She had told me — at length, with a pony in it — that her wishes came true sideways and a day late and she didn't always mean them. She'd told me about the snow in July. And I'd nodded along and never once put together that the weather was her.

So the moment wasn't going to end. She'd seen to that. I was in it with her, in the same puddle — she'd have said that, she talked like that — and whether I'd been kidnapped or rescued was by now a fairly academic question.

In the morning the rain had not stopped. Of course it hadn't. The lot was a lake, the frond was still in the door, and when the guard let a woman with a stroller in and I came up behind her, he put his arm across the doorway without looking at me and waited till I'd stepped back.

Stella was awake before me. She was sitting a little way off with a cupcake in her hand she wasn't eating, and she was looking at me — not the dog look. A new one. Careful. The look you give a plate of something you've been told is good.

"You've got something," she said. "On your face."

She didn't wipe it. Yesterday she'd have wiped it. Yesterday she'd have wiped it without asking and gone the colour of a stop sign. She looked at the icing on my chin, and at her own hand, and then away, at the graham crackers, and I watched her make herself look back.

And I understood what I'd done, and I want to be clear that my first feeling was not remorse. It was outrage. *Her?* She didn't get to find *me* disgusting. She had two fingernails on the floor and mascara in the crease of her eye and she'd eaten the paper. I had wished, through her, to be disliked by everyone, and I'd been looking straight at her while I did it, and thinking of the guard and the girls with scissors and the whole turning-headed world, and I had not, for one second, counted her in it. She'd been the only person who'd ever said she understood me and I'd wished it off her like an eyelash.

"Do you still —" I said, and couldn't finish it. I'd have given the poster back to the guard's daughter. I'd have given them all back.

"I'm working on it," she said.

Which was more than I could say. I never had.

## Chapter Nine

I woke up first, and I looked at him, and there was nothing there.

I'll be honest about it, because I told him on this floor last night that I would be, and this is the part I'd leave out if I could. He was asleep on his side with his mouth open and an arm over his face, in the sweatpants, with the icing, with a bit of hair stuck to his cheek, and I looked at him the way I'd looked at him ten thousand times — in the interviews, from across the street, from behind the parked car — and I waited for the thing to happen. The lift. The buzz. It didn't come. What came instead was a small, clear, mean thought in a voice I recognised as mine: *what a strange-looking man.*

Then: *what is he doing on a floor.*

Then — this is the one — *he's not even that tall.*

I sat up so fast I knocked the cupcakes over.

You have to understand what I'd been, before him. When I was nine a girl said a mean thing about raspberries and I blew on a dandelion, and the next day there were paper strawberries hanging from the ceiling of my classroom, and I decided — nine years old, in a plastic chair — that I would never want anything again. Not a little. Not on the inside where no one could see. Because wanting, for me, wasn't wanting. It was doing. So I had the paper candles and the shitty college and I let the dress go by and I walked around for fifteen years with my hands in my pockets so I wouldn't touch anything, and it was fine. It was safe. Nobody died.

He was the one thing I'd let myself have. A face on a screen, a man who laughed at nothing — it had seemed so small. So harmless. A mini cupcake's worth of wanting. And I'd made the one wish in seven years, and it had brought me here, to a floor, with him, and I'd wanted him so hard and so long that I'd forgotten what the other thing felt like. The nothing. The safe.

And now here it was again, on the morning after the one wish I'd ever made for somebody else. The wish had gone out to find everyone who might dislike him, and I had forgotten that I was someone.

And here is the worst part, the part I'd really leave out: it was a relief. Hands back in my pockets. Nothing to knock over. Nine years old again and safe.

I got the candles out of the bag while he slept. I got the matches, the little box that opened like a drawer, and I set a cupcake on the linoleum and pushed a candle into it and sat there with a match in my hand and did not strike it.

Because Samantha didn't come back. The snow didn't un-snow. Every wish I've ever made has come true crooked, a day late and sideways, with something knocked over on the way, and a wish to want him again would come true crooked too. I'd want him too much, or wrong, or I'd want him and he'd be gone, and it would be exactly as unearned as the pony. I'd have wished my way into loving him the way I'd wished my way into everything.

And the other way — the way with no candle — was the thing I'd sworn off at nine. To want him on purpose. With my hands out of my pockets. Knowing what my wanting does.

I looked down the aisle. It went on and on, both directions, rows and rows of it, everything a person could want, in packets, and no way out of the building. That's a supermarket. I'd never thought about it before.

I put the match back in the drawer.

I started with the laugh. I could want the laugh. That was one.

He woke up. He saw the cupcake with the candle in it and the box of matches in my hand, and his face did something — it went young and frightened and then blank on purpose, which I'd seen him do once, in the fish place, when the waitress goggled.

"Don't," he said.

"I'm not."

"You were going to."

"I was going to. I'm not."

He looked at the candle. He looked at me, and I made myself look back — it was like holding a door open with your foot — and I said, "You've got something on your face," and I did not wipe it, because I wanted to, a little, and I didn't trust the wanting yet.

"Do you still —" he said.

"I'm working on it."

Outside, the rain kept on. It was going to keep on; I'd seen to that, without meaning to, the way I do everything. The frond was in the door and the guard was under the awning with his back to us, and there was nowhere to go, and twenty-one cupcakes, and a man I'd have to learn to want from scratch, on purpose, on a floor, in the same puddle, for a time.

Savior or captor. I'd said it didn't make any difference. It turns out it's the same job either way: you stay.
