# Robert Pattinson — the ending (chapters 8–9), draft 1

Picks up after chapter seven's last line ("Thank you," he said, a smile playing at his lips). Chapter 8 is Robert, chapter 9 is Stella — the same handoff the draft already makes at "Prove it."

## Chapter Eight

Nothing happened.

That was my first thought, and I'll admit it was a disappointed one. The candle went out — she'd blown it — and the little curl of smoke went up and joined the general Ralphs air, and the rain kept on and the door kept banging and the guard at the front kept being the guard at the front, and I was exactly as I had been. I checked. I held my hands up in front of my face in the light from the freezer cases. Same hands. Same long, famous, ridiculous fingers.

"Well," I said.

"It doesn't — it's not always right away," she said. "Sometimes it's a day. Sometimes it's —"

"A week, yes, you said."

"Or sometimes it's already happened and you just don't know yet."

I ate the cupcake. It seemed the thing to do. It was terrible — it tasted like the cardboard it came in, sweet cardboard — and I ate the whole thing in three bites and licked the paper and felt, for reasons I could not have explained, extremely cheerful.

We slept in the aisle. I want to say that plainly, because it was the strangest night of my life and I have had some strange ones. The guard came by at some point and looked down at us — the movie star and the girl in the frock, on the floor between the juice boxes and the graham crackers, with twenty-three cupcakes — and he opened his mouth, and I braced for it. The Cullen thing. The daughter thing. The phone coming out.

He shut his mouth. He looked at me a moment longer with an expression I couldn't read — like he'd smelled something, but was too polite to say so — and he went away.

"Did you see that," I whispered.

She was asleep. She slept with her mouth open and one hand flat on the linoleum, palm up, like she was waiting for someone to put something in it.

In the morning the rain had stopped and the parking lot was a lake with a palm frond floating in it. The doors were working again. The guard held one open for a woman with a stroller and let it swing shut on me, and I stood there with my hand on the glass and laughed so hard I had to sit down on the ice machine.

It was true. It was all true, and it was wonderful. I walked out into the parking lot without my cap and without my glasses and a man loading his trunk looked up at me and looked straight back down, the way you look away from someone who's about to ask you for money. A girl of about fifteen — the exact age, the precise demographic, I could have picked her out of a catalogue — walked past me so close her bag brushed my arm, and she pulled the bag in, and made a face. A small one. The face you'd make at a smell.

"Excuse me," I said to her. I couldn't help it. "Do you know who I am?"

She looked at me. She took her time. "No," she said. "Should I?" And she went into the Ralphs, and I stood in the lake in the sweatpants and felt the sun on my actual face for the first time in — I did the maths — nine years.

Stella was waiting by the car. She had the cupcakes. She'd found, somewhere, a napkin, and she'd wet it, and she reached up and wiped a bit of pink icing off my chin without asking and then went that colour again and looked at the ground.

That was when I understood what she'd done wrong. Or what she'd done — I still don't know if it was wrong.

She'd wished it for everyone, and she'd meant everyone. But she'd been looking at me when she said it, and she was the one person on earth it had nothing to work on, because she had already seen the sweatpants and the barista and the waistband man and the — the whole of it, the disgusting whole of it — and she'd driven me to a fish place anyway.

So: disliked by every person on the planet, and it was glorious, and I was going to ruin it, I could feel it coming — by being liked by exactly one. The one I'd spent the whole day being disgusting to.

"Get in," she said, quietly, to the ground. "I'll take you home."

## Chapter Nine

He didn't try the door.

That's the thing I keep coming back to. The whole drive down the coast, the whole hour of it, with the waves on the right (I checked, this time; they were on the right) and the sun coming up over the hills the way it does after rain, like it's embarrassed about something — he sat in the passenger seat with his hands in his lap and the window down and his hair going everywhere, and he did not once try the door.

I'd unlocked it. I'd unlocked it in the parking lot, deliberately, with the button, so he'd hear it. Chunk. So he'd know.

He put his feet up on the dashboard. He put them down again when I looked at him. Then he put them back up.

"You could drop me at the café," he said. "My glasses are there."

"Okay."

"Or not."

"Okay."

He laughed at that — at nothing, at "okay" — the real laugh, the one from the interviews, the one that folds him in half. It went on longer than it should have. Everything did, with him. He was a person who couldn't do anything the normal amount.

"Stella," he said, when he'd finished. It was the first time he'd said it. I hadn't been sure he'd caught it, at the fish place; I'd only said it once, and quietly. "You know it didn't work on you."

"I know."

"Do you know why?"

I did know why. I'd known on the floor, with the candle going, before I'd even finished saying it. There's a thing that happens when I wish — a sort of turning-away, like the wish goes out to look for everyone it's meant for — and I'd felt it go out, and I'd felt it come back round and find me and not know what to do with me. I was already done. There was nothing in me for it to change.

"Because you're stubborn," he said, before I could answer, and grinned at the windshield.

"That's not —"

"I know that's not why."

There was the café. Robert Patterson was putting the chairs out. I slowed down, and I put my signal on, and I looked at him — Robert, the other Robert, mine — and he was looking at the café too, at the table by the door where his glasses were, and his cap, and his whole other life, the one where nobody would ever look at him twice again. He could have it. All he had to do was get out.

"Keep going," he said.

"Where?"

"I don't know. I don't — anywhere. You've got twenty-three cupcakes in the back. We'll think of something to want."

So I kept going. Past the café, past the barista with his chairs, and Robert leaned out of the window as we went by and shouted "PATTERSON!" and waved, and Robert Patterson looked up and, without a flicker, went back to his chairs.

I have never in my life seen anyone so happy to be ignored.
