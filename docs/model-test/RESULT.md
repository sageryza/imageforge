### 1 · pc-01 (was 1)
**Kicker:** Protection & Cleansing
**Heading:** The oldest magic
**Body:** Long before anyone wrote down a love spell, people were scratching marks beside their doors and burying charms under their floors. The craft has two halves, and this lesson teaches both: **warding**, which keeps trouble out, and **cleansing**, which clears out what already got in.

### 2 · pc-02 (was 2)
**Kicker:** The idea
**Heading:** Guard the thresholds
**Body:** Folk magic treats a house like a body: trouble gets in through the openings. So the old charms cluster at doors and windows, and above all at the **chimney**, the one opening nobody could shut. Ward the crossing points and you have warded the house.

### 3 · pc-11 (new — needs art)
**Kicker:** The marks
**Heading:** Witch marks
**Body:** Look closely at the beams of an old English house or church and you may find them near a door or a hearth: daisy wheels drawn with a compass, two interlocking Vs, a scorch made on purpose with a candle flame. Historians call them **apotropaic marks**, from the Greek for *turning away*. In 2016 Historic England asked the public to report them, because nobody had any idea how many there were.

### 4 · pc-03 (was 3)
**Kicker:** The purifier
**Heading:** Salt
**Body:** Salt preserves meat, draws water out of a wound and keeps rot away, so tradition after tradition trusted it against the unseen as well. Lay a thin line across the doorway, a pinch in each corner of a room, or a small bowl by the door to soak up anything heavy. *Empty the bowl outside, away from the house, never into your own bin.*

### 5 · pc-04 (was 4)
**Kicker:** The steadier
**Heading:** Iron
**Body:** Across Britain and Ireland, folk belief held that fairies and ill-wishers alike could not abide **cold iron**, so knives went under doorsteps and nails into doorframes. The horseshoe over the door is what survives of that. Most people hang it **points up** so the luck can't pour out, though blacksmiths are said to hang theirs points down to pour luck onto the anvil.

### 6 · pc-05 (was 5)
**Kicker:** The trap
**Heading:** The witch bottle
**Body:** Around two hundred have been dug up in England, most of them from the 1600s: a stoneware jug packed with bent pins, nails, hair, nail clippings and the owner's urine, sealed and buried under the hearth or the threshold to catch any curse aimed at the household. In 2004, builders in Greenwich found one that was still sealed, with everything still inside.
**ⓘ FAQ:**
- **Why urine and hair?** — The bottle stands in for the person. Something of theirs draws the ill will in, and the pins and nails pierce it where it lands.
- **Did people really believe in them?** — Enough to write them up. The astrologer-physician **Joseph Blagrave** gave instructions for one in his *Astrological Practice of Physick* (1671).
- **Do witches still make them?** — Yes. A modern one is a jar of pins, nails, rosemary and something of yours, sealed tight and buried by the door, or kept in a dark corner of the house. The rule has never changed: once it is sealed, it stays sealed.

### 7 · pc-06 (was 6)
**Kicker:** The sweep
**Heading:** The broom is a tool
**Body:** A broom moves energy as well as dust. Sweep **toward the door and out** (in Hoodoo tradition, from the back of the house to the front) so whatever is stale leaves the way visitors come in. Between sweeps, many hang it by the door with the bristles up, standing guard.

### 8 · pc-07 (was 7)
**Kicker:** The cleansers
**Heading:** Smoke & water
**Body:** Scottish households kept a New Year rite called **saining**. Water drawn from a river ford was sprinkled through the house, then juniper was burned in every room with the windows shut until the smoke drove everyone coughing, and then the doors were flung open. The same two streams still do the work today: **smoke** of rosemary or juniper carried out an open window, and **salted water** wiped along the sills and doorframes.

### 9 · pc-08 (was 8)
**Kicker:** The practice
**Heading:** A house cleanse
**Body:** Start with what the cleanse is for: after an illness, after a row, after a guest who left a mood behind, or before you move in somewhere new. Open the windows first so there is a way out. Sweep toward the door, carry smoke into the corners where heaviness collects, and wipe the sills with salted water. Then close up, light one candle, and say out loud what you cleared.

### 10 · pc-12 (new — needs art)
**Kicker:** The ward
**Heading:** Setting a ward
**Body:** A cleanse clears a house, and a **ward** keeps it clear. Once the air has settled, set one: a line of salt at the threshold, iron above the door, rosemary on the sill, or a mark of your own drawn on the inside of the frame. Many witches renew theirs each new moon or at every turn of the season.

### 11 · pc-09 (was 9)
**Kicker:** On your person
**Heading:** Little armor
**Body:** Protection travels. An old rhyme runs *"Rowan tree and red thread / put the witches to their speed"*: a rowan twig bound in red thread, carried in the pocket. Today's versions include a charm bag of salt and rosemary, a key worn as a pendant, or a red thread tied at the wrist.

### 12 · pc-10 (was 10)
**Kicker:** After
**Heading:** Start at your own door
**Body:** **Tonight:** set one ward on your front door, either a line of salt along the threshold or a nail or horseshoe above it, and write down what you used and why. Once that is second nature, the next step is a full house cleanse, and after that a witch bottle of your own. ✦

## Notes for review

- **The two biggest changes.** The witch marks card and the ward card are new, because the old deck taught cleansing and never actually showed how to set a ward. The last card also changed from "then rest easy" to a next step, per the aspirational rule.
- **Removed:** "Protection is the first magic every culture invented", which overclaimed. "The old house-cleansing charms all end with the words spoken aloud" is gone too, because I couldn't back it up. "A well-warded home is simply a place your shoulders drop" read as a pronouncement.
- **Facts to check before shipping:** Historic England's 2016 witch-marks appeal · about 200 English witch bottles, the sealed 2004 Greenwich find · Blagrave 1671 · saining (water from a ford, then juniper smoke) · the rowan-and-red-thread rhyme (the Scots original reads "gar the witches tyne their speed") · the blacksmiths' points-down horseshoe, which is written as hearsay.
- **Voice sweep:** no mic-drop closers, no "isn't X — it's Y" reframes, no therapy verbs, no permission-granting, no consolation. The safety line on the salt bowl is kept.
