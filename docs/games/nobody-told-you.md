# Nobody Told You

*A party game for 4–8. Telephone Pictionary crossed with exquisite corpse,
except everybody at the table is secretly obeying a rule, and at the end you
try to work out what everyone's rule was.*

Sophie's idea, Aug 2026. This is the first written version — untested at a
real table. The open questions are at the bottom.

---

## The one-paragraph version

You each draw a secret rule card and keep it for the whole game. Then you play
telephone Pictionary: a sentence becomes a drawing becomes a sentence becomes a
drawing, each person seeing only the panel directly above them. Every mark you
make must obey your rule. At the end you unfold all the strips at once and try
to name each other's rules — which is very hard, because by the time your rule
reached the fourth panel it was being applied to something three other rules
had already mangled.

## What you need

- A long strip of paper per player (a till roll, or letter paper turned
  landscape and folded into 6–8 panels). One panel per player, plus one for
  the seed.
- A pen each.
- The rule deck (below). Print it, or write the rules on index cards.
- A timer.

## Setup

1. Deal one rule card to each player, face down. **Read it, don't show it,
   keep it for the entire game.** It does not change between rounds — that is
   what makes deduction possible at all.
2. Everyone takes a strip and writes their name on the back.
3. **Write a plain seed sentence in the top panel — no rule applied.** Any
   sentence: *"A man is late for a wedding."* The seed is the one honest row
   on the table, and it is the only place anybody gets to see uncontaminated
   material. Fold it back so only that panel shows, and pass left.

## The rounds

Everybody works at the same time, on the same kind of panel, then passes left.

- **Odd passes are drawings.** You can see only the sentence above. Draw it.
  ~90 seconds.
- **Even passes are sentences.** You can see only the drawing above. Write what
  you think it shows. ~45 seconds.

Before you pass, fold so that only *your* panel is visible to the next person.

**Every panel you make must obey your rule** — every drawing, every sentence,
all game. Not "when it fits." Every time. (There is a scoring penalty for
chickening out; see the Compliance Check.)

With N players you play **N−1 passes**, so every strip is touched once by
everybody and comes back to its owner still folded. Your rule ends up
fingerprinted on N−1 strips — that is the whole evidence budget the table gets.

## The reveal

Unfold every strip at once and lay them out side by side. Read them out. Enjoy
this part; it takes as long as it takes.

Then, **before anyone says a word about rules**, everybody secretly writes down
a guess for every other player's rule. One line each.

## The Compliance Check

Go around the table. One at a time, each player names their rule out loud and
then **points at every single panel they made and shows where the rule is.**

This is the load-bearing rule of the whole game. Without it the winning
strategy is to pick the most invisible rule you can and apply it as feebly as
possible, and the game dies of cowardice. So:

> **A player who cannot point to their rule in every panel they made scores
> nothing as a rule-holder.** The table votes if it's close, and the table
> should be a bit harsh about it.

## Scoring

As a **seeker**, for each other player:

- **3 points** — you named their rule in substance. Wording doesn't have to
  match; the table votes on near-misses.
- **1 point** — you were warm: right shape, wrong specifics. *("Always adds an
  animal"* against *"always adds a bird"* is warm, not correct.)

As a **rule-holder**, if you passed the Compliance Check:

- **1 point** for every player who failed to name your rule, to a maximum of 2.

The hider cap is deliberately low. Being caught should cost you almost nothing,
so that playing your rule boldly and legibly is never the wrong move.

Highest total wins. Ties are fine — nobody has ever cared who won this.

## The rule deck

Every card has to work on **both** a drawing and a sentence, because you never
know which kind of panel is coming. ○ = plain, ● = sneaky. A first game should
be dealt from the ○ cards only.

**Add something**

- ○ There is always a bird.
- ○ There is always a hand that doesn't belong to anybody.
- ○ Something is always on fire.
- ○ There is always weather.
- ○ Somebody is always eating.
- ● There is always something that shouldn't be indoors.
- ● There is always exactly one thing that is brand new.

**Take something away**

- ○ You never show a face.
- ○ Nothing you make has the ground in it.
- ○ You never use a word longer than five letters.
- ● Nothing in your panel is bigger than a person.
- ● Nothing you make contains a straight line.
- ● You never depict motion. Everything is stopped.

**Change something**

- ○ Everything is one size larger than it should be.
- ○ The most important thing becomes food.
- ○ Everything has aged fifty years.
- ○ Left and right are swapped.
- ● The most important thing is made of the wrong material.
- ● Whatever you receive, you move it outdoors.

**Change the mood**

- ○ Yours is always sadder than what you received.
- ○ Yours is always more polite than what you received.
- ○ Yours always looks more expensive than what you received.
- ● Yours is always more crowded than what you received.
- ● Somebody in yours is always about to leave.

**Rules about the panel itself**

- ○ There is always something in the top-left corner.
- ○ There are always exactly three of something.
- ● You always contradict one detail from the panel above.
- ● Your panel is always divided in two.
- ● You always repeat one thing from the panel above, exactly.

Twenty-nine cards, which is enough that a group of six can play four or five
games before anything repeats.

## Why it's nearly impossible, said out loud

This is the point of the game rather than a flaw in it, but it's worth being
honest about the mechanism, because it's what the scoring has to survive.

Your rule is applied to material that has already been through one to five
other rules. *"There is always a bird"* enters the strip on panel two; by panel
three somebody has read the bird as the subject of the picture and written a
sentence about a bird; by panel four somebody whose rule is *"the most
important thing becomes food"* has turned it into a roast chicken. The bird is
now evidence of the wrong rule, pointing at the wrong player. Signal doesn't
decay across the strip so much as it gets **re-attributed**, which is worse and
much funnier.

So the deduction is not really "spot the rule." It is "spot the rule that
explains the most panels once you've accounted for what everyone else did to
them," and nobody at a party is going to do that properly. The partial-credit
scoring exists to convert that from a frustration into a game: a table that
never scores a single 3 still has everyone scoring 1s all night.

## Variants

- **The ghost.** Deal one blank card. That player has no rule and behaves
  normally. At the reveal everyone also names who they think the ghost was;
  the ghost scores 3 for every player who doesn't. Adds paranoia and makes
  every genuinely uneventful panel suspicious.
- **Two cards.** For a table that's played a few times. Both rules, every
  panel. Scoring unchanged, guesses are per-card.
- **The long game.** Keep the same rule card across several sessions on
  different nights. By the third night people start recognising each other's
  handwriting, which is the good ending.
- **All drawings.** No sentence rounds at all. Faster and much harder.

## Open questions for the first real playtest

Genuinely untested — these are the places I'd expect it to break.

- **Is N−1 passes too long?** Six players is six panels of work each. It may
  want to cap at 5 passes regardless of table size.
- **Do the sneaky ● cards ever get guessed?** If nobody ever names one across
  a whole game they may just be dead weight, and the deck should be all ○.
- **Is 90 seconds enough to draw *and* apply a rule?** Suspect not for the
  transformation cards. May need 2 minutes for draws.
- **Does the Compliance Check drag?** Eight players × six panels each is a lot
  of pointing. Might need to be spot-checked — two panels, table's choice —
  rather than exhaustive.
- **The seed being plain** is a call I made for a clean baseline. The other
  option is that your seed obeys your rule too, which is thematically nicer
  (nothing on the table is ever honest) but makes several cards impossible to
  satisfy from scratch — you can't *remove* the largest object from a sentence
  you're inventing.
